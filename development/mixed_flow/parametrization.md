# An engineering parameterization of the meridional channel

## Recommendation and source rationale

Use three independent layers:

1. **Meanline skeleton:** inlet radii, outlet radius and width, axial length, and
   inlet/outlet inclinations. These determine station positions and sizes.
2. **Wall geometry:** separate hub and shroud tangent angles, inlet/outlet tangent
   reach, and optional bow. These determine turning between fixed stations.
3. **Blade meridional extent:** separate leading/trailing-edge positions on each
   wall, with optional edge bow and splitter placement. These trim a region inside
   the fixed channel.

This closely follows Casey's separation of channel geometry and blade location
([1983 paper](<papers/Casey - 1983 - A Computational Geometry for the Blades and Intern.pdf>),
p. 292, Figs. 5–6) and the four groups of engineering inputs in
[Casey & Robinson (2021)](<papers/Casey and Robinson - 2021 - Radial flow turbocompressors design, analysis, an.pdf>),
§14.4.5, pp. 475–478, Fig. 14.6. Their internal Bézier coordinates are derived
from engineering parameters. The book uses different polynomial orders for
hub/shroud; the present implementation uses one common smooth family to simplify
the API and preserve zero endpoint curvature.

Romei uses cubic Bézier walls and varies wall slopes, blade sweep, and splitter
start. Its strongest transferable result is the usefulness of these controls,
rather than a unique optimal curve. See the [separate review](romei_design_guidelines.md)
for the quantitative findings and the limits of transferring near-critical CO₂
results to other compressors.

## Coordinates, stations, and inclinations

Coordinates are $(z,r)$: $z$ increases axially and $r$ increases away from the
shaft. All angles are in degrees from $+z$ towards $+r$. Thus $0^\circ$ is axial
and $90^\circ$ is radial. `inlet_flow_angle_deg` and `outlet_flow_angle_deg`
define nominal station-normal directions; they do not calculate a velocity field.

The inlet radii $r_{1h},r_{1s}$ are **actual endpoint radii**, even for an inclined
inlet. Let $\Delta r_1=r_{1s}-r_{1h}$ and let the inlet midpoint have $z=0$.
For inlet inclination $\theta_1$,

$$
H_1=\left(\tfrac12\Delta r_1\tan\theta_1,\ r_{1h}\right),\qquad
S_1=\left(-\tfrac12\Delta r_1\tan\theta_1,\ r_{1s}\right).
$$

The inlet connector is normal to $(\cos\theta_1,\sin\theta_1)$, and its length is
$b_1=\Delta r_1/\cos\theta_1$. Changing inclination at fixed inlet radii changes
inlet area; use the area adapter below if area must remain fixed.

The outlet midpoint is $O_2=(L,r_2)$, where $L$ is the axial distance **between
station midpoints**, not the hub length. With normal width $b_2$,

$$
n_2=(-\sin\theta_2,\cos\theta_2),\qquad
H_2=O_2-\tfrac12b_2n_2,\qquad S_2=O_2+\tfrac12b_2n_2.
$$

At a radial exit, $z_{2h}=L+b_2/2$, $z_{2s}=L-b_2/2$, and both radii equal $r_2$.
At an inclined exit, $r_2$ denotes the midpoint radius, not the largest tip radius.

This centrifugal/outward mixed-flow template currently requires
$0<r_{1h}<r_{1s}<r_2$, $L>0$, $b_2>0$,
$-90^\circ<\theta_1<90^\circ$, and $0^\circ<\theta_2\le90^\circ$.
Both outlet endpoints must have positive axial coordinates and radii. Exactly
radial inlet, inward-flow machines, and a purely axial outlet are outside this
template. In particular, distinct inlet endpoint radii cannot describe an exactly
radial inlet section. The supported angles satisfy the requested inclined inlet
and outlet use case; extreme combinations can still produce invalid channels.

## Baseline dimensions and meanline connection

| Input | Baseline | Meaning |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| `outlet_radius` | 0.100 m in the example | Global scale; supply from meanline |
| `inlet_hub_radius` | $0.25r_2$ | Actual inlet hub radius |
| `inlet_shroud_radius` | $0.65r_2$ | Actual inlet casing radius |
| `outlet_width` | $0.08r_2$ | Width normal to nominal outlet flow |
| `axial_length` | $0.60r_2$ | Inlet-to-outlet midpoint axial spacing |
| `inlet_flow_angle_deg` | $0^\circ$ | Axial inlet |
| `outlet_flow_angle_deg` | $90^\circ$ | Radial outlet |

Only axial length and inclinations have defaults in the dimensional constructor;
the four radii/width inputs remain explicit. `MeanlineDimensions.from_ratios()`
provides the illustrative complete baseline. **The selected ratios, length,
and shape defaults are proposed starting values, not correlations or CFD optima.**
The $0.60r_2$ length is not the Aungier correlation cited by Romei.

When meanline supplies dimensions, pass them directly. When it supplies mass
flow, density, and representative normal velocity, obtain geometric area using

$$
A_i=\frac{\dot m}{\rho_i V_{n,i}(1-B_i)},
$$

where $B_i$ is the chosen fractional area blockage. Omit $(1-B_i)$ for an
unblocked model; do not apply blockage twice if the upstream meanline convention
already includes it. `from_flow_areas()` accepts **geometric areas**, without
performing thermodynamics or inferring blockage. With inlet hub/tip ratio $q$,

$$
r_{1s}=\sqrt{\frac{A_1\cos\theta_1}{\pi(1-q^2)}},\qquad
r_{1h}=q r_{1s},\qquad b_2=\frac{A_2}{2\pi r_2}.
$$

```python
dimensions = MeanlineDimensions.from_flow_areas(
    inlet_area=0.011309733552923256,  # example geometric area [m²]
    outlet_area=0.005026548245743669,
    outlet_radius=0.100,
    inlet_hub_to_shroud_ratio=0.025 / 0.065,
    inlet_flow_angle_deg=0.0,
    outlet_flow_angle_deg=90.0,
)
```

These formulas agree with Casey & Robinson §11.6.5, p. 366, Eq. (11.43) for the
radial outlet without blockage. Shaft sizing, material/stress limits, density,
relative inlet Mach optimization, speed, and slip/deviation remain upstream design
tasks. They are not hidden inside a wall-shape function.

## Independent wall controls

Each `WallShape` has the following inputs, independently for hub and shroud.

| Input | Default | Engineering interpretation |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| `inlet_angle_deg` | `None` | Inlet wall tangent from $+z$; `None` inherits inlet station angle |
| `outlet_angle_deg` | `None` | Outlet wall tangent from $+z$; `None` inherits outlet station angle |
| `inlet_tangent_fraction` | 0.45 | Fraction of the chord projection used for inlet tangent reach |
| `outlet_tangent_fraction` | 0.45 | Fraction of the chord projection used for outlet tangent reach |
| `bow` | 0 | Mid-polynomial normal displacement divided by chord length |

Larger tangent reach generally extends the influence of the endpoint direction;
it is a straightness/turn-distribution control, not a guaranteed straight segment
length or prescribed bend radius. Inlet and outlet reaches can be tuned separately.
Positive bow displaces the wall towards the chord's left normal $(-D_r,D_z)$;
for the baseline this is upstream and radially outward. It is not always a pure
radius or passage-width increase.

Suggested first sensitivity ranges are reach fractions 0.25–0.65 and bow
$-0.035$ to $0.035$. These are examples, not established design limits. The code
allows fractions $0.01\le f\le1$; the lower bound prevents nearly degenerate
tangents from defeating the fixed arclength table. Endpoint wall angles are
restricted to $(-90^\circ,180^\circ)$ and must have positive chord projections.
The channel checks may reject a combination even when each scalar is in range.
No arbitrary aerodynamic bound is imposed on bow; excessive bow can fold a wall
or channel and is rejected by the geometry checks.

### Internal polynomial and exact B-spline representation

For wall endpoints $P_0,P_5$, chord $D=P_5-P_0$, and unit endpoint tangents
$t_0,t_1$, compute engineering tangent reaches

$$
\ell_0=f_0(D\cdot t_0),\qquad \ell_1=f_1(D\cdot t_1).
$$

The implementation derives the internal control points:

$$
P_1=P_0+\tfrac12\ell_0t_0,\quad P_2=P_0+\ell_0t_0,\quad
P_3=P_5-\ell_1t_1,\quad P_4=P_5-\tfrac12\ell_1t_1.
$$

They are not user input variables. The wall is

$$
W(u)=\sum_{i=0}^{5}\binom5i(1-u)^{5-i}u^iP_i
       +64u^3(1-u)^3 b(-D_r,D_z),\qquad 0\le u\le1.
$$

Here $b$ is `bow`. At $u=0.5$ the additional displacement has magnitude
$|b|\lVert D\rVert$. The bow and its first two derivatives vanish at both ends,
so all shape changes preserve endpoints, endpoint tangents, and zero endpoint
curvature. Straight ducts can therefore be joined with geometric curvature
continuity; such ducts are not generated by this implementation. A nonzero
prescribed endpoint curvature would require extending this family.

Without bow this is a quintic Bézier curve, equivalently a single clamped quintic
B-spline. With bow it is degree six. `WallCurve.as_bspline()` returns an **exact**
clamped degree-six SciPy `BSpline` in both cases: degree-elevate the quintic and
add $3.2b(-D_r,D_z)$ to its middle coefficient. The knot vector is seven zeros
followed by seven ones. There is no fitting approximation. The spline uses $u$,
not the arclength coordinate described next.

This deliberately compact family is suitable for baseline studies. Multiple
localized shape features would be better added as extra spline spans with
engineering controls than by exposing arbitrary control-point coordinates.

## Channel coordinates and geometric diagnostics

Each wall is reparameterized by its own normalized physical arclength,

$$
s(u)=\frac{\int_0^u\lVert W'(v)\rVert\,dv}{\ell_w}.
$$

The code integrates speed with a 4097-point Simpson table and uses monotone cubic
interpolation for inversion. Polynomial evaluation and its first/second
derivatives are analytic; the arclength inversion is numerical.
`point(u)` and `point_at(s)` therefore have deliberately different names.

For $\eta=0$ at the hub and $\eta=1$ at the shroud,

$$
C(s,\eta)=(1-\eta)H(s)+\eta S(s).
$$

Equal fractional wall arclength is a geometric correspondence, not a solved
streamline field. Interior $s$ is not generally normalized physical arclength
of the interpolated curve. Stations are straight spanwise connectors and need
not remain normal to the local streamwise direction.

The exact area swept by revolving a connector is

$$
A_{cut}(s)=\pi[r_h(s)+r_s(s)]\,\lVert S(s)-H(s)\rVert.
$$

This gives $A_1=\pi(r_{1s}^2-r_{1h}^2)/\cos\theta_1$ and
$A_2=2\pi r_2b_2$ at the nominal stations. `projected_area` projects this cut onto
the midpoint tangent $t_m$:

$$
A_{proj}=\pi(r_h+r_s)\det[t_m,S-H].
$$

It is only a geometric estimate of normal flow area. Density, spanwise velocity
variations, blade blockage, and boundary layers are absent. Neither the minimum
cut area nor its projection is the blade-passage throat; see Casey & Robinson
§11.5, pp. 355–357. The baseline itself has a small area minimum near the exit
before the area rises to $2\pi r_2b_2$; this is exposed in the diagnostics.

Construction checks finite inputs, positive radii, nonvanishing wall speed, and
positive channel Jacobian

$$
J=\det[C_s,C_\eta]>0.
$$

The Jacobian is affine in $\eta$, so testing its hub and shroud values checks all
span fractions at each sampled $s$. By default there are 1001 streamwise samples;
`channel.validate(n=5001)` increases resolution. These checks screen local
folding/collapse; they are not a mathematical guarantee of global injectivity
between samples or a CAD-quality intersection certificate. Diagnostics return
wall coordinates, signed curvature, connector width, both areas, and boundary
Jacobians. There is no universal maximum curvature imposed by the generator.

## Independent main-blade and splitter extents

`BladeEdges` specifies four wall arclength fractions: `leading_hub`,
`leading_shroud`, `trailing_hub`, and `trailing_shroud`. Defaults are 0, 0, 1, 1,
so the nominal meanline stations coincide with the blade edges. Each edge also
has an optional bow $b_e$:

$$
s_e(\eta)=(1-\eta)s_{e,h}+\eta s_{e,s}+4b_e\eta(1-\eta),
\qquad e\in\{LE,TE\}.
$$

Bow is an extra streamwise coordinate at midspan, not an axial length, lean
angle, or circumferential displacement. An edge with zero bow is linear in the
$(s,\eta)$ plane and can be curved in physical $(z,r)$ coordinates. For the
baseline, placing the hub LE farther downstream than the shroud LE gives
positive meridional sweep. A physical endpoint-to-endpoint sweep angle can be
measured using
$\gamma=\operatorname{atan2}(z_{LE,h}-z_{LE,s},r_{LE,s}-r_{LE,h})$;
it is an output of this construction, not an independent input that also fixes
both edge endpoints. A future angle-input adapter can solve for one endpoint.

The blade extent is

$$
M(\xi,\eta)=C\left(s_{LE}(\eta)+\xi[s_{TE}(\eta)-s_{LE}(\eta)],\eta\right),
\qquad 0\le\xi\le1.
$$

Exact quadratic extrema enforce $0\le s_{LE}<s_{TE}\le1$ over the entire span,
including bowed interiors. Its Jacobian is $(s_{TE}-s_{LE})J$, so valid edge
ordering preserves the orientation of an already valid channel map.

`blade.splitter(start_fraction=0.25)` retains the main TE and puts the splitter
LE at $s_{LE}+0.25(s_{TE}-s_{LE})$. This is exactly 25% of the main blade's
wall-arclength extent at hub and shroud; in the interior it is 25% of the mapped
$s$ interval. It is not guaranteed to be 25% of physical arclength at every
intermediate span. This convention must be checked when comparing with Romei's
normalized meridional-distance definition. Independently swept splitters can
instead be constructed as another `BladeMeridional(channel, BladeEdges(...))`.

Changing blade edges leaves the wall curves unchanged. However, moving LE/TE
away from the nominal stations changes the **actual blade inlet/outlet radii,
width, and wall tangents**. The CSV and summary exports report actual edge
coordinates. Reconcile these locations with meanline sizing before evaluating
performance. The current model does not automatically preserve the nominal
blade-eye area under a sweep change.

The casing curve is also not the blade tip surface of an open impeller with
clearance. Keep clearance as a later blade trim, consistent with Casey & Robinson
§14.5.2, p. 479. Circumferential camber, thickness, lean/rake and blade count can
subsequently be added over this meridional domain.

## Suggested refinement sequence

1. Supply accepted meanline dimensions and station directions. Review shaft and
   casing constraints before refining curves.
2. Start with inherited wall angles, reach fractions 0.45, and zero bow. Inspect
   width, area evolution, and curvature; change axial length if turning is too
   concentrated.
3. Tune wall inlet slopes independently. Adjust outlet slopes and outlet tangent
   reach to control the final turn and area gradient. The book explicitly notes
   the sensitivity to outlet casing slope (§14.4.5, p. 477).
4. Add small wall bow only if the existing controls cannot address a local shape
   issue. Compare the whole curve rather than treating bow as a local radius.
5. Position blade LE/TE and splitters independently, then inspect the actual
   resulting edge dimensions. For a Romei-inspired radial case, try hub inlet
   $10^\circ$, shroud inlet $0^\circ$, hub outlet $90^\circ$, shroud outlet
   $85^\circ$, and compare splitter starts 0.20 and 0.25. This does not reproduce
   Romei's wall family or coupled inlet sweep construction.
6. Evaluate the resulting blade geometry with throughflow/CFD and mechanical
   constraints, including the operating range. The geometry module itself
   cannot establish whether a refinement improves performance.

Casey & Robinson §11.2, p. 340 suggests an inlet casing diameter below about
$0.73D_2$ for radial machines, while mixed-flow machines can exceed this. Treat
it as a review guide, not a hard geometry bound. Likewise the typical
$w_2/w_{1c}$ diffusion range 0.5–0.7 in §11.6.4, p. 366 belongs in aerodynamic
assessment; the present model has no velocities with which to enforce it.

## Verification and references

The tests exercise exact endpoint areas at inclined and radial stations, wall
angles and zero endpoint curvature, analytic derivatives, independent numerical
arclength integration, exact B-spline equivalence, geometric scaling, separate
blade/splitter placement, and invalid channel/edge combinations. Baseline and
inclined example scripts also run end to end. These establish geometric
behavior, not compressor performance.
